What four rounds of research came to

The right pond, in the right place, filled by rain.

Every word in that sentence is load-bearing. Most ponds are the wrong shape, half of them never fill, a great many are filled from a borewell rather than the sky, and the thing that decides whether one was worth digging is a decision taken after the digging is finished.

The issue

A city that floods, then runs dry

Chennai does not have a shortage of rain. It has a shortage of places to put it.

Day Zero

On 19 June 2019 city officials declared Day Zero. All four main reservoirs had effectively run dry. Combined, Poondi, Cholavaram, Red Hills and Chembarambakkam hold 11,057 million cubic feet. That June they held about 0.1 per cent of normal capacity.

About 11.2 million people went without drinking water. Around 820,000 people living in slums were in areas the tankers could not reach. Some families received 30 litres a day. Government tankers sometimes took a month to arrive after being asked for.

The cause was not a single failure. Monsoons failed in 2016, 2017 and 2018 in succession. The 2018 northeast monsoon gave Chennai 343.7 mm against a normal of 757.6, a deficit of 55 per cent.

Fifty-four days

Chennai gets about 140 centimetres of rain a year, across roughly 54 rainy days. Tamil Nadu takes 48 per cent of its annual rainfall in the three months of the northeast monsoon, October to December. Chengalpattu takes 61 per cent in those three months.

Figure 1. Where Chengalpattu’s rain falls. The district takes 707.7 mm of its 1,166.8 mm in October, November and December, and the remaining 459 mm spread across the other nine months. The three columns inside the monsoon block are the measured 2023 season, officially recorded as 4 per cent above normal, which contained a 43 per cent deficit, a 28 per cent surplus and a 43 per cent surplus, in that order. The problem is not how much falls. It is that it all falls at once, and never the same way twice.

The state's own rainfall record puts it plainly: "the intensity of rainfall is high during north east monsoon." Heavy rain over hard, dry ground runs off rather than soaking in. That is the water a farm pond is trying to catch.

What a rain-fed farm is

Rain-fed means no canal and often no working well. The whole crop depends on rain arriving at the right time. Near Chennai the rain-fed rotation is groundnut, then millets, then pulses: cumbu, ragi, green gram, black gram and red gram.

Red gram is the one to watch. In Thiruvallur district there are 1,869 hectares of it unirrigated against 289 irrigated. It is also the crop with the largest recorded response to a single rescue irrigation: in Maharashtra field surveys it went from 1.5 quintals an acre to 5.

So the question this project is really asking is narrow and practical. Can one well-timed irrigation, from water caught in October, save a crop in March?

The ground underneath

About 73 per cent of Tamil Nadu is hard rock. Groundwater sits only in the weathered layer on top of it, which averages 10 to 15 metres deep. Wells deeper than about 12 metres go dry between May and July.

Statewide, 392 of 1,202 firkas are over-exploited, meaning more groundwater is taken out each year than goes back in. Chengalpattu firka itself went from 87 per cent of recharge in 2013 to about 107 per cent in 2024.

And the number that changed how we think about this whole project: only 40 per cent of Tamil Nadu's groundwater recharge comes from rain falling on the ground. The other 60 per cent is seepage out of tanks, canals and irrigated fields. The aquifer is refilled more by water that has already been somewhere than by rain soaking in where it lands.

Farm ponds

A hole, and everything that decides whether it works

A farm pond is a pit dug on one farmer's land to catch runoff. Simple to describe, and almost every part of it can be got wrong.

The parts that matter

A pond that works has four things most ponds do not have. A silt trap upstream, so soil settles out before it reaches the water. An inlet that brings runoff down without cutting the bank. An outlet set about 30 cm below the inlet, so a big storm leaves safely instead of bursting the wall. And freeboard, a margin of about 20 per cent between the full water level and the top of the bank.

A survey of about 300 ponds in one Maharashtra village found that none of them had the inlets and outlets the national scheme guidelines require.

The Indian Standard for lined farm ponds, IS 15828, specifies film thickness, jointing, anchoring and soil cover in detail. It contains no silt trap requirement, no maintenance procedure, and no safety or fencing provision at all, in a country with 38,503 drowning deaths a year.

Does it work?

Most of them do not

Start with the number nobody puts on a poster.

Figure 4. One hundred ponds. In a survey of 188 households and 206 ponds across six villages of Sangamner block, Ahmednagar, almost 70 per cent were non-functional. The authors call it dead investment. Most of the ponds they measured were two to three times larger than the guidelines recommend, and many were built with no technical advice at all.

The same study adds something worse. If every abandoned pond were brought back into use, regional water losses would rise sharply, because each working pond means more surface evaporating and more groundwater pumped to fill it. Fixing them all would make the water problem worse.

This is one study, in one block, of one district, in a different state. There is no equivalent figure for Tamil Nadu, and we could not find one.

Against it: a government study of 100 ponds in each of Anantapur, Adilabad and Chittoor found every one of them economically viable, with benefit-to-cost ratios of 2.7 and above. Those ponds were selected for study, and the paper does not say how.

Both things can be true. Ponds that are used pay for themselves. A large share are not used.

Published seepage rates for sandy clay loam span 2.4 to 29 centimetres a day, a factor of twelve, because most of them are canal figures rather than pond figures. We use the Rahuri extension rule of 24 mm a day for sandy clay loam, which is the most conservative and the only one that matches field experience, and we scale clay and sandy ground from it using the ratios in the canal table only, not its absolute numbers. So 10 mm a day for clay and 43 for sandy ground are our construction, not anyone’s measurement. Lining reduces seepage by up to 95 per cent. One thing the figure cannot show: a newly dug pond leaks far more than an old one, and trials at Dehradun and Rajkot found seepage falls to almost nothing after about eight years of silting, so a farmer who gives up after one dry season may be giving up several years early.

We argue against lining, because seepage recharges the shallow aquifer that dug wells depend on, and because the plastic is 70 per cent of the cost.

The strongest case for lining comes from the people who promote the technology: "Black soils may not need lining for farm ponds, while red soils need lining to retain water." The ground around Chennai is red and sandy. On their own guidance, we should line.

We think they are right about the engineering and have not considered the aquifer. But that is our judgement, and a reader should know it is contested.

The bigger picture

Tamil Nadu already solved this once

Sixteen thousand tanks, built with mud and manpower, each one overflowing into the next. Then they were allowed to silt up.

The cascade

Tamil Nadu has no perennial rivers. Centuries ago that was solved by cascading tanks, called eris, where each tank's overflow fed the one below it down a chain. A man called the neerkatti watched the levels and channelled water to individual fields, paid with a share of the crop. The whole village repaired the tank together, a practice called kudimaramathu.

Figure 7. A cascade. Scroll and each tank overflows into the next. Then they silt up, one by one, and the chain stops reaching the end.

Kancheepuram district, next door to our school, still has 1,942 tanks irrigating over 60,000 hectares. Chengalpattu has 152 on the register. Tamil Nadu's tanks together hold a reported 347 TMC, which would be about thirty times the storage of the four reservoirs that supply Chennai.

What happened when they failed

The International Water Management Institute compared wells around 17 restored tanks against conditions roughly twenty years earlier. It is the clearest thing we found in four rounds of research.

Read it as a sequence. The tanks silted up. The shallow aquifer they recharged stopped being recharged. Dug wells halved. So farmers drilled, and the borewells went seventeen times more numerous and four times deeper.

Tank irrigation in Tamil Nadu fell from 38 per cent of the net irrigated area in 1960-61 to 14 per cent in 2022-23. The IWMI team's verdict on the tanks they visited: none of them gets sufficient water in eight years out of ten.

A working farm pond is the cheapest water in the system by a wide margin. At the real failure rate it costs about the same as recycled sewage. So the sentence is not ponds are cheap. It is ponds are the cheapest thing available, and only if they work, and most do not.

Which points the money at maintenance rather than construction. Tamil Nadu's 2026-27 budget funds 500 new ponds and the desilting of 500 existing ones, for Rs 8.5 crore. In the same period the state is spending about Rs 500 crore restoring 3,260 tanks. Roughly fifty-nine times more on repairing old storage than on digging new.

How to claim the farm pond subsidy in Tamil Nadu

One page. What is on offer, who qualifies, which office, and what to take with you.

What is on offer

Farm ponds on patta land are subsidised at 90 per cent under most Tamil Nadu schemes, and at 95 per cent for SC and ST farmers under TN IAMWARM. The beneficiary pays the remaining 10 per cent, and may pay it in cash, labour or material.

In the 2026-27 agriculture budget the state funded 500 new farm ponds at 100 per cent subsidy, and the desilting of 500 existing ponds at 75 per cent using departmental machinery.

What you can do

The useful things are smaller than you think

Most of what helps is maintenance, not construction. That is less exciting and more effective.

If you are a student in Chennai

Mission Blue-Green Chengalpattu is restoring about 200 of the district's 500 identified tanks, and 388 more are open to voluntary organisations. The Environmentalist Foundation of India has committed to 100 of them in this district alone. They take volunteers.

That is the single highest-leverage thing available to someone our age here, because a restored tank recharges the shallow aquifer that everyone's wells sit in.

If you run a school

Tamil Nadu made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory in every building in 2003. A 2015 audit found government buildings had got it entirely wrong, with broken and clogged pipes.

Checking whether your own building's system still works costs nothing and will probably find a blockage. The structure existing and the structure working are different things.

If you are planning a pond

Three things from the research. Build it square and no deeper than the soil allows, because shape decides how much survives to May. Put in a silt trap, the cheapest part and the one that decides how long the pond lasts. And fence it, which no Indian scheme requires.

Then plan what you will plant with the water before you dig, not after.

What we are not asking for

We are not raising money. We considered building a pond ourselves and dropped it, because the point of this project was never to construct one piece of infrastructure. It was to find out whether the thing everyone recommends actually works, and to say so honestly.

We are also not telling anyone that farm ponds are the answer. On the evidence we found, the honest version is narrower: the right pond, in the right place, filled by rain, on land no tank reaches.

Our research

What is wrong with what we found

Every site like this tells you what it discovered. This page tells you where our evidence is weak. It is the most useful page here.

How we worked

Four rounds of reading, about 29,000 words of notes, 44 sources. We went to the primary document wherever we could: the Central Ground Water Board's 2024 assessment of Tamil Nadu, the Indian Standard for lined farm ponds, India Meteorological Department monsoon reports, and the Current Science paper that compares the best and worst performing ponds in three districts.

Everything on this site is secondary research. The interviews, the pond visit and the surveys have not happened yet. When they do, this page changes.

Eight things wrong with our evidence

  1. The sources lean towards their own promoters. Most technical literature comes from ICAR, CRIDA and state agriculture departments, all of which have reasons to report that farm ponds work. The critical literature is a much smaller set from partly overlapping networks.
  2. Selection is never explained. The Current Science study reports that "100 farm ponds were selected" in each district. Selected how? If through extension officers who knew which farmers were doing well, the sample is not the population.
  3. Publication bias runs one way. Ponds that worked get written up. Abandoned ponds get counted only when somebody sets out specifically to count them, which has happened approximately once.
  4. Almost nothing is from Tamil Nadu. The only peer-reviewed study of farm pond performance in this state is a cotton trial at Kovilpatti, 600 km south of us with a quarter of our rainfall. Every runoff coefficient and evaporation rate on this site is borrowed from somewhere else.
  5. Contradictions we could not resolve. Published seepage rates for sandy clay loam span 2.4 to 29 cm a day, a factor of twelve. Tamil Nadu's two official climate projections disagree on whether rainfall will rise or fall. Chengalpattu's baseline rainfall appears as both 1,166.8 mm and 1,215.5 mm in two publications from the same institution.
  6. A peer-reviewed number that does not check out. The Current Science paper states that deepening a pond by one metre cuts exposed area by about 65 per cent. Working it through the geometry gives 8 to 11 per cent. We think the paper is wrong by roughly a factor of six.
  7. The money is not comparable. Costs here come from 2008, 2013-14, 2017, 2024 and 2026, in rupees of the day, with no inflation adjustment.
  8. No farmer speaks here unmediated. Every farmer view on this site has been through a researcher's survey instrument and come out as a score.

What changed our minds

We started believing farm ponds were straightforwardly good. Three findings moved us.

That in one Maharashtra village, 75 to 80 per cent of ponds were filled from borewells rather than rain, turning a rainwater harvesting structure into a device for moving groundwater somewhere it evaporates faster.

That when 320 farmers were asked why they had abandoned their ponds, the reason they ranked first was draining the well to fill the pond. The criticism is not something outsiders imposed. The people who built them worked it out and stopped.

And that 60 per cent of Tamil Nadu's groundwater recharge comes from seepage out of tanks, canals and fields rather than rain soaking in. Which means lining every pond, at scale, would work against the mechanism that keeps the aquifer alive.

Still open

  • No measured runoff coefficient exists for the Chennai hinterland under the northeast monsoon.
  • Nobody has published what a farm pond costs to build in Chengalpattu under current departmental rates.
  • Nobody appears to have asked whether Tamil Nadu's farm ponds are borewell-filled the way Maharashtra's are. That is a genuinely open question and we intend to ask it.
  • No source anywhere breaks drowning deaths down by water body, so there is no figure for farm pond drownings. The gap itself is a finding.
